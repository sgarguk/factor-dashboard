## Executive Summary

The remainder of 2026 is a fight between two forces that are both true at once: **earnings delivery is genuinely strong and broadening**, and **the macro regime underneath it is less benign than the equity tape suggests**. The Fed has now held rates five consecutive meetings at 3.50–3.75%, but for the first time since 2016 three regional presidents dissented *for higher rates*, and the June dot plot moved the median 2026 projection up, not down. The FOMC's own July minutes named the AI investment boom, the Middle East conflict, and tariff policy as the three live drivers of inflation risk — that is now a mainstream policy concern, not a fringe call.

At the same time, Big Tech is delivering: Magnificent 7 earnings growth of roughly 32% in Q2 versus ~26% for the rest of the S&P 500 — the narrowest gap since 2023 — with Microsoft's Azure, Amazon's AWS, and Nvidia's data-center revenue all accelerating. But the AI capex bill is now real money: ~$725bn of 2026 hyperscaler capex (up 77% YoY), a Street consensus that 2027 tops $1 trillion, and a genuine, BIS-flagged debate about circular financing, shortening effective asset lives, and off-balance-sheet lease commitments. Meta's Q2 print — a revenue beat with an EPS miss, free cash flow collapsing from $8.5bn to $784mn, and capex guidance raised again — is the clearest single data point of the tension this report is built around.

**Key calls for H2 2026:**

1. **Inflation is the swing factor for every asset class this half.** Base case is core inflation holding in the high-2s to low-3s with a live tail risk of a break above 4% if tariff pass-through, AI-capex input costs (memory/HBM prices have doubled), and fiscal-deficit-driven demand combine. The Fed's own dissent structure means "no more cuts" is now more likely than further easing; a 2026 hike cannot be ruled out.
2. **Equities: momentum still working, but breadth is thinning.** The median S&P 500 stock sits ~13% below its 52-week high even as the index sits near highs — a divergence Goldman flags as historically preceding larger-than-average drawdowns. Seasonality (May–October, midterm year) argues for defensiveness into Q4.
3. **Fixed income: bear-steepener, not bull-steepener.** Short rates easing on Fed accommodation, long rates rising on fiscal/term-premium concerns — the opposite of the recession-signal steepening investors are trained to expect. Duration extension is a hedge against a growth shock, not a rate-cut trade.
4. **Gold and the dollar are both being pulled by the same de-dollarization/fiscal-dominance thread**, even as gold has round-tripped from a January all-time high near $5,590/oz down through $4,000 and back to the low $4,000s.
5. **Our own pairwise Donchian rotation signal has already made this call.** The 7-ETF model rotated out of pure Momentum (SPMO) into Dividend Growth (DGRO) on 16 July 2026 — a defensive tilt that lines up with the breadth-divergence and rate-hike-risk story below. See §8.

## 1. Macro Backdrop

### 1.1 Inflation — sticky, reaccelerating in pockets, one shared shock

- **US:** Core inflation spiked to 3.2% YoY in March 2026 (highest since 2023) on tariff pass-through and the Iran/Middle East oil shock. It has since been volatile: June 2026 CPI printed **headline -0.4% MoM, core flat 0.0% MoM, core YoY 2.6%** — a downside surprise driven by a sharp energy reversal, not a durable disinflation trend. Core PCE is running closer to **~2.8%**, still the Fed's real obstacle. Fed research estimates near-full pass-through of tariffs to consumers (average effective tariff rate **~6.6%, the highest since 1969**, costing the average household ~$900/year). Some independent forecasters flag upside risk to **>4% core by year-end** if fiscal deficit expansion (>7% of GDP), a tight labor market, and AI-capex demand spillovers (memory-chip prices have **doubled** since mid-2025) combine.
- **Eurozone:** Headline HICP eased to **2.8% YoY in June** (from 3.2%), but this is a base-effect illusion — **core climbed to 2.6%** and **services inflation accelerated to 3.5%**, both worse than May. Eurosystem staff project headline inflation averaging **3.0% in 2026**.
- **UK:** CPI 2.6% (June)/2.8% (May); services inflation 3.7%; BoE sees a brief push above 3.25% in Q4 on the same energy shock.
- **Japan:** Inflation and yen weakness together forced the BOJ into its most aggressive hiking cycle in 30 years (§1.2).
- **China:** Outright deflationary — March CPI **-0.1% YoY**; full-year forecasts cluster at just 0.6–1.2%.

**The shared driver across regions is the 2026 Iran/Middle East war and its Strait-of-Hormuz disruption** (Brent briefly >$100/bbl; IMF called it the largest oil-supply disruption in the history of the market), layered on top of region-specific tariff and AI-capex input-cost pressure. This is a genuine, identifiable shock — not a diffuse "inflation is just sticky" story — which matters for how transitory markets should treat it.

### 1.2 Central banks — the hike-risk debate is now real

| Bank | Policy rate | 2026 move | Signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal Reserve | 3.50–3.75% (held 5th straight meeting, 29 Jul) | 9–3 vote; 3-way **hawkish** dissent for higher rates — first since Sept 2016 | June dot plot revised median 2026 projection **up** from 3.4%→3.8%; 9 of 19 officials now see ≥1 more hike |
| ECB | Hiked **+25bp in June** | Reversal from easing cycle, citing war-driven inflation | Staff still project 3.0% inflation in 2026 |
| BOE | Held at 3.75% (7–2 vote, two dissents for a hike to 4.00%) | Cuts seen unlikely rest of 2026 | Forecast range across economists: 3.50–4.25% |
| BOJ | Hiked to **1.00%** in June — highest since 1995 | 7–1 vote, further hikes signaled | Yen firmed modestly to ~160/USD post-move |
| PBOC | On hold | Shifted from easing to "wait-and-see" | Deflation persists; transmission impaired by property sector |

The market-pricing framing has genuinely shifted in 2026: January minutes showed officials willing to consider hikes if inflation stayed above target; by July, the FOMC's own minutes named **three explicit drivers of inflation risk — the AI investment boom, the Middle East conflict, and tariff policy** — and delivered the first three-way hawkish dissent in a decade. As of late July, market pricing sits around **54% for one more 25bp cut**, i.e., the live debate is "one more cut vs. hold," with hike risk sitting underneath as a tail the FOMC itself is now voicing.

### 1.3 Growth — decelerating, not (yet) recessionary

- **Global (IMF, July WEO update):** 3.0% for 2026, 3.4% for 2027 — but IMF's own adverse/severe scenarios (oil sustained >$100 through 2027) show growth falling to 2.5% (adverse) or ~2.0% (severe) with inflation >5–6%. This is a real, modeled downside tail, not boilerplate risk language.
- **US:** Q2 2026 GDP came in at **1.5% annualized**, down from 2.1% in Q1 — a genuine deceleration. Goldman sees H2 cooling further to 1.25–1.75% "stall speed" even while keeping a 2.8% full-year forecast; recession-probability estimates range from Goldman's ~20% to JPMorgan's 35%.
- **China:** 4.5% growth target, defended by a **>4%-of-GDP fiscal deficit** (widest in decades) — the growth lever is fiscal, not monetary, since the PBOC has stopped easing.
- **Europe:** EC's own spring forecast cut 2026 growth to **0.8%** (from 1.4% in 2025), explicitly blaming the energy-price shock extending into 2027.

### 1.4 Fiscal dominance and sovereign debt sustainability

This is the theme that connects rates, gold, the dollar, and the "bond vigilante" language back into circulation in 2026. US FY2026 deficit is tracking **~$1.9–2.0 trillion (~5.8–5.9% of GDP)** — Treasury borrowed $602bn in just the first three months of the fiscal year. CBO's 10-year path: debt held by the public reaching **100.4% of GDP by end-FY2026**, ~120% by 2036. **~$10 trillion of Treasury debt requires rolling over in 2026**, and some auctions have shown weaker demand around geopolitical stress episodes.

The notable shift in 2026 is that "fiscal dominance" has moved from an academic/fringe framing to a term used explicitly and convergently by **PIMCO** ("Rupture and Resilience" — a *structural break*, not a cyclical wobble), **BlackRock** (reviving "financial repression" as a plausible policy tool), **Bridgewater** ("Modern Mercantilism" — central banks pushed toward "passive compliance," a possible debt-death-spiral tail risk, and a reported ~15% gold-allocation thesis tied directly to this view), and the **IMF** itself (warning of a "drift toward fiscal dominance" eroding central bank independence). When four institutions with materially different books all reach for the same language in the same year, that is itself a signal worth weighting.

### 1.5 Geopolitical risk map

- **Iran war / Strait of Hormuz** — the dominant 2026 shock. Conflict broke out 28 Feb 2026; a fragile US–Iran ceasefire from 8 April has been repeatedly tested, with Iran alleging violations into July. This is simultaneously an oil shock, a gold/safe-haven driver, an agricultural-commodity shock (fertilizer +31% forecast for 2026), and a core-inflation driver across every region in §1.1 — treat it as the single largest swing factor for H2 positioning, not a contained regional event.
- **US tariff policy** — Supreme Court struck down IEEPA-based tariffs in Feb 2026, but USTR finalized a new broad Section 301 tariff regime (23 July) covering 80+ trading partners; effective tariff rate now ~6.6%, the highest since 1969.
- **US midterms (Nov 2026)** — base case is a split Congress; consensus view is this is equity-neutral-to-favorable and unlikely to force a major policy pivot, though tariffs remain the single biggest swing issue for the election itself.
- **AI capex as a systemic risk** — the BIS's June 2026 Annual Report explicitly names AI-capex-bust, circular-financing collapse, and sovereign-debt fragility among the top global financial-stability risks. This is no longer just an equity-valuation debate; it is being framed as a macro-financial-stability issue by the institution that tracks systemic risk for central banks.
- **Yen carry-trade unwind** — BOJ hiking into a ~$500bn estimated outstanding carry-trade base is a standing tail risk for global risk assets, not unique to Japan.

## 2. Global Equities

### 2.1 US equities — levels and valuation

S&P 500 ~7,416 (+~9% YTD); Nasdaq ~+7.8% YTD; Dow ~+8% YTD. Forward P/E ~20.1x — above the 5-year average (19.9x) and 10-year average (19.0x), well below the dot-com peak (23.5x). The rally has real earnings underneath it (e.g., Microsoft +~12% on its print day), which is the key difference from a pure multiple-expansion melt-up — but the multiple is not cheap, and it is being asked to absorb a genuine rate-hike-risk narrative simultaneously.

### 2.2 Big Tech earnings and the AI capex debate

**Q1/Q2 2026 highlights:**

| Company | Result | Capex signal |
|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | Revenue $81.3bn (+17% YoY, beat); Azure +38–39% cc; Cloud revenue >$100bn annualized | Q2 capex $35.8–37.5bn; FY26 trending to **~$190bn**; $80bn Azure backlog **power-constrained**, not demand-constrained |
| Meta | Revenue $60.8bn (+28%, beat); **EPS $6.18 vs $7.17 expected (miss)** | Q2 capex $31.1bn (nearly 2x Q2'25); FY26 guidance raised to **$130–145bn**; FCF collapsed **$8.5bn → $784mn**; new $14bn/1GW Texas data center with BlackRock |
| Alphabet | Revenue +21% to $113.6bn; EPS beat expectations | Capex raised again |

**Scale of the capex cycle:** Combined 2026 hyperscaler capex (Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta) is tracking **~$725bn, up 77% YoY**; adding Oracle and other names pushes some estimates past $800bn; the Street now broadly expects **2027 to top $1 trillion**. One framing worth flagging to the CIO directly: 2026 hyperscaler capex as a share of US GDP is now argued by several analysts to exceed the peak spending intensity of the Manhattan Project, Apollo program, the interstate highway system, and the dot-com fiber buildout, combined on a like-for-like basis.

**The bear case is no longer fringe:**
- **Depreciation mismatch** — critics argue 5–6 year depreciation schedules on GPU/CPU assets understate a true 2–3 year economic life, understating cumulative depletion by an estimated **$176bn over 2026–2028**.
- **Off-balance-sheet exposure** — Moody's flags **~$662bn** in signed-but-not-commenced data-center lease commitments sitting outside GAAP balance sheets.
- **Circular financing** — Nvidia is reportedly in discussions (unconfirmed, early-stage) to support up to **$600bn** in OpenAI financing (a $250bn Ohio data center + $350bn in GPU purchases), which critics call circular: Nvidia effectively financing the revenue it books from its own customer. Bloomberg has mapped a broader web of these circular deals across Microsoft/OpenAI/Nvidia. OpenAI is reported to generate ~$2bn/month in revenue against large operating losses.
- **Named bear:** Michael Burry has increased short positions in Nvidia and Micron in 2026, calling the AI narrative "mass addiction," explicitly citing the BIS 2026 report and drawing a 1999–2000 dot-com parallel. BofA's Bubble Risk Indicator puts semiconductors at 0.91 — a near-bubble reading.
- **Power, not demand, is the binding constraint** — Microsoft's $80bn unfulfilled Azure backlog is the cleanest evidence that the bottleneck has shifted from GPU supply to electricity supply, which feeds directly into the copper and nuclear/gas themes in §4 and §6.

**Our read:** the bull case (broadening earnings, genuine revenue growth, self-funded-from-operating-cash-flow framing) and the bear case (circular financing, shortening asset lives, power constraints, off-balance-sheet leases) are **both currently true**. Meta's Q2 print — beat on revenue, miss on EPS, FCF collapse, capex raised anyway — is the single cleanest illustration of this tension available in the current earnings season, and is worth using as the go-to example in client/committee conversations about AI capex risk (subject to Compliance review before any external use).

### 2.3 Earnings breadth — genuinely improving

Mag 7 earnings growth of ~32% vs. ~26% for the rest of the S&P 500 in Q2 is the **narrowest gap since 2023** — real broadening, not just narrative. Full-year 2026 S&P 500 EPS growth consensus estimates range from ~15% (FactSet) to ~24% (Goldman, implied by its $340 EPS estimate) — a meaningful dispersion across providers that likely reflects different estimate vintages rather than a real disagreement, and should be reconciled against a single dated source before being quoted externally. Within the ~15% consensus figure, roughly 5.4pp comes from the Mag 7 and ~8.9pp from the rest of the market — a genuine narrowing of the concentration problem versus 2024–2025.

### 2.4 Momentum and technicals — thinning breadth under a strong headline tape

VIX ~18–20 (up from ~16.6 a week prior) — elevated caution, not crisis. The more important signal: **the median S&P 500 stock sits ~13% below its 52-week high even as the index trades near highs** — Goldman's equity strategy team flags this breadth-divergence level as one that has historically preceded larger-than-average 6–12 month drawdowns. There is a live sell-side disagreement on what this means (Sevens Report reads it as a market-top warning; BTIG reads it as a positioning unwind, not a genuine rotation signal) — treat as a real, unresolved technical debate rather than a settled call. Seasonality compounds this: May–October, and especially midterm-election years, is historically the weakest, most drawdown-prone window, with volatility risk seen peaking into November.

### 2.5 Developed ex-US

**Europe:** STOXX 600 ~649, tracking toward a "modest positive" full-year total return (~8%, Goldman) on ~5% EPS growth; DAX outlook (~+9.7%) is explicitly supported by Chancellor Merz's rearmament/infrastructure stimulus — a genuine, identifiable fiscal tailwind rather than generic optimism. Banks, financials and tech are the flagged sector leadership.

**Japan:** Nikkei 225 hit an all-time high of **62,833.84 on 7 May 2026**, having fully erased its 1989 bubble peak in Feb 2024 (+61% since). Valuation is rich — Nikkei ~22.7x forward, TOPIX ~16.7x, both >1 standard deviation above 10-year averages — with investors reportedly more focused on technical/trend strength than on the BOJ's tightening cycle as a de-risking trigger. That is itself worth flagging as a complacency signal given the BOJ has explicitly said more hikes are coming and an estimated $500bn of yen-funded carry trades remains outstanding.

### 2.6 Emerging markets

**China:** 2026 GDP target 4.5–5%, defended by fiscal stimulus (bond issuance +CNY 1 trillion) rather than monetary easing; property policy aims for "stable, controlled adjustment" rather than aggressive rescue; an "anti-involution" policy is trying to curb destructive price wars in tech/manufacturing. Goldman expects the China equity rally to continue but at a slower pace, favoring new-economy/AI-supply-chain exposure over old-economy property/banking names.

**India:** A weak 2025 (Nifty 500 +6.7% INR, badly lagging EM's +30%) sets up a 2026 re-rating thesis — Goldman is constructive citing policy support and improved valuations, with 2026 earnings growth expected around 16%. Near-term, Indian equities were down five straight sessions into late July on elevated oil prices and Iran-war risk — a direct read-through from §1.5.

**Broad EM:** Reported MSCI EM performance shows +25.6% YTD through late May followed by a sharp pullback into July — a swing large enough that it may partly reflect index/currency-basis differences across sources rather than a single clean number; treat the direction (strong H1, volatile H2 entry) as more reliable than the precise magnitude.

### 2.7 Factor and style performance — a fast-rotating regime

YTD through Q2: **High Beta** and **Small Cap** led (+8.6%, +6.5%), **Value** stayed resilient (+2.8%, 2025's leaderboard topper), and **Momentum reclaimed factor leadership in Q2** after Value led Q1 — JPMorgan and Certuity factor commentary both flag this Value→Momentum handoff explicitly as a fast rotation, not a stable regime. Growth and Dividend factors were the weakest YTD performers in most datasets. Non-US stocks reportedly outperformed US stocks in every factor category except momentum — i.e., the "AI/momentum" leadership is a specifically US phenomenon this year.

## 3. Fixed Income & Credit

### 3.1 Yield curve — a fiscally-driven bear steepener

2yr ~4.14%, 10yr ~4.68% (2026 high), 30yr ~5.18% (2026 high). The curve has exited its long 2022–2024 inversion and is steepening through 2026 — but this is a **bear steepener**: short rates easing on Fed accommodation while long rates rise on fiscal/term-premium concerns, the opposite of the classic recession-signaling bull steepener. Consensus expects fed funds drifting toward ~3.8–4.0% by year-end and further curve steepening as long yields stay elevated on deficit/term-premium dynamics even as short rates ease.

### 3.2 Credit — tight spreads, record issuance, bifurcated defaults

IG OAS ~77–80bp (near multi-decade tights); HY OAS ~285bp (well inside the 20-year average of ~490bp) — several strategists flag this combination as a **complacency signal**. Gross IG issuance is on pace for a record **~$2.25tn in 2026**, eclipsing the prior 2020 record, driven by a >$1tn refinancing wall and AI-capex-related debt. Defaults are bifurcated: speculative-grade bond defaults easing toward ~3.8% by year-end, but **leveraged loan defaults running 7.3–7.9%** (Moody's) — the real stress is concentrated in lower-rated/floating-rate credit, not broad-based.

### 3.3 Duration — a genuine split among major managers

Schwab favors shortening duration; AllianceBernstein recommends holding duration explicitly as an equity-risk hedge; Vanguard sits neutral, preferring credit selection over rate calls. The underlying tension is structural: sticky inflation argues against extension, while a fiscally-driven steepening argues for duration's hedge value if a growth shock actually materializes.

### 3.4 Emerging market debt — no systemic stress, idiosyncratic pockets

Sharp Q1→Q2 reversal: EMBIGD returned -1.26% in Q1 (spreads +35bp) before tightening 53bp to 235bp in Q2 as the US-Iran ceasefire framework unwound the geopolitical risk premium; HY sovereigns outperformed IG sovereigns materially (+6.74% vs +2.48%). Argentina was upgraded (Fitch, B- from CCC+); frontier sovereigns (Argentina, Egypt, Nigeria, Ukraine) now represent ~26% of the JPM EM sovereign index — a structural shift toward higher-beta exposure within the benchmark itself.

### 3.5 Balance sheets, term premium, cash

Fed QT formally ended 1 December 2025 (balance sheet ~$6.7tn, down from an $8.9tn peak — only about half of pandemic-era growth reversed); ECB continues QT (~€500bn of securities returning to market in 2026). Term premium has risen off 2020 lows but remains below long-run average — "reasserting," not yet extreme — though "bond vigilante" language (Yardeni's term) is back in mainstream circulation via OMFIF, Goldman and others, tied to the fiscal-dominance theme in §1.4. Cash/T-bill yields (~3.7–4.0%) remain attractive on a standalone basis but carry reinvestment risk if the Fed does eventually resume cutting.

## 4. Commodities & FX

### 4.1 Gold — round trip, structurally supported

Spot ~$4,060–4,130/oz, down ~5–7% YTD after an extraordinary 2025 (+60%+, 50+ record highs) and a **January 2026 all-time intraday high near $5,590/oz**, before falling below $4,000 in late June and recovering into the low $4,000s. Two structural demand drivers are doing more work than the traditional real-yield channel (which has arguably broken down — gold has rallied even through periods of *positive* real yields): **central bank buying** (>700 tonnes in 2025, the largest annual increase since 1967; gold has **surpassed Treasuries' share of central bank reserves for the first time since 1996**; 45% of surveyed central banks plan to add more gold over the next 12 months) and **AI-bubble hedging demand**. Year-end forecasts are unusually dispersed — from Goldman's now-bearish $4,900 to JPMorgan's $6,300 — which itself signals how contested the "is this still a buy" question is at current levels.

### 4.2 Oil — a war premium riding on top of a structurally oversupplied market

WTI ~$84.6, Brent ~$90.5 (both highly volatile intra-month, having ranged from ~$69/$72 up to >$100 during acute war phases). The Iran war (28 Feb outbreak, fragile 8 April ceasefire repeatedly tested since) is the dominant 2026 story — IEA called the Strait-of-Hormuz disruption the largest supply shock in the oil market's history, with global supply down 10.1mb/d in March. Separately, and importantly, **OPEC+ is on its own supply-normalization path** (fifth consecutive monthly output increase from August), and Goldman's structural, non-war-adjusted view has oil averaging materially lower (WTI ~$52, Brent ~$56) on a persistent ~2mb/d global surplus. **The read: absent the war premium, the underlying oil market is oversupplied and bearish — the entire 2026 price story is a geopolitical overlay on top of a fundamentally weak base case, which is a very different risk profile than a fundamentally tight market with a war premium on top.**

### 4.3 Copper — the clearest AI-capex-to-commodity transmission line

~$6.44/lb (COMEX), having spiked above $14,500/tonne intraday earlier in 2026 on an "AI metals frenzy" colliding with supply shocks. The demand math is concrete: **a single 1GW AI "data center factory" requires ~50,000 metric tons of copper**; at a projected ~15GW/year of new AI data-center capacity, that is **~750,000 tonnes of incremental annual demand** before counting grid/transmission buildout, which several analysts argue is an even larger copper driver than the data centers themselves. Structural deficits are projected at 124,000 tonnes (2026) and 150,000 tonnes (2027). This is one of the most direct, quantifiable links between the AI capex theme in §2.2 and a specific tradeable commodity.

### 4.4 US dollar and FX

DXY ~100–101, having touched a 4-year low near 97 earlier in 2026 before rebounding on near-term rate-differential support. Every major bank forecaster (Goldman, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, ING, MUFG, Deutsche Bank) expects DXY to end 2026 **below** current levels — the disagreement is entirely about magnitude and path, not direction, and the structural bear case is explicitly tied to the fiscal-deficit/de-dollarization theme running through §1.4 and §4.1.

EUR/USD ~1.14, GBP/USD ~1.33. **USD/JPY is the pair to watch**: bank forecasts diverge sharply between continued yen weakness (Goldman/MUFG/JPMorgan targeting 165) and a stronger yen (ING 153, Scotiabank 150) — the US-Japan rate gap is narrowing for the first time in years even as it remains wide enough (>250bp) that a single 25bp BOJ move doesn't automatically break the carry trade. 160 is the flagged historical MOF intervention trigger. Separately, **INR has depreciated 15–17% against CNY** over the past year — a larger move than INR's depreciation against USD, the first time that's happened since 2013, with a direct, quantifiable cost impact on Indian importers sourcing from China.

### 4.5 Broader commodities, CTAs, digital assets

Agricultural commodities are up sharply (Bloomberg Ag Index +13.4% YTD) on the same Iran-war shipping disruption plus El Niño risk; fertilizer prices are forecast +31% for 2026. Managed futures/CTA performance remains genuinely regime-dependent — strong in 2026's episodic volatility (one representative fund +23.9% 1-year) but structurally reliant on sustained, directional trends rather than the choppy, event-driven volatility that has characterized 2026 so far. Bitcoin (~$64,000) sits meaningfully below most institutional year-start targets ($80k–$250k range), and is down materially year-over-year even after a recent bounce on the Fed's hold — direct evidence that neither gold nor Bitcoin is currently delivering a clean "pure flight-to-safety" trade despite the AI-bubble and geopolitical backdrop that would normally favor both.

## 5. Alternatives

**Private equity:** Fundraising is up 9% YoY in dollar terms but concentrated into fewer, larger established managers; deal count is down 67% while aggregate deal value is up ~10% — sponsors concentrating capital into fewer, higher-conviction deals. **DPI (realized distributions) has become the defining 2026 fundraising metric**, as LPs push back on years of unrealized NAV marks. Continuation vehicles now represent ~20% of PE distributions — an increasingly default liquidity mechanism in place of true exits, which raises its own valuation/conflict-of-interest questions.

**Private credit:** The Proskauer Private Credit Default Index rose to 2.73% (from 1.84% two quarters earlier); BDC retail capital formation is down 40% YoY, the sharpest contraction on record; PIK-toggle usage is rising as a share of investment income — a classic "extend and pretend" signal. Framed by several trackers as the sector's "most challenging environment since the 2008 GFC," though this is concentrated in floating-rate/near-term-refinancing borrowers rather than the asset class broadly.

**Real estate:** Sharply bifurcated. Office CMBS delinquency is at **12.34%**, above the 2008 GFC peak, with ~$875bn of CRE loans facing a 2026 maturity wall — a structural (remote/hybrid work, obsolescence), not cyclical, distress story. Residential is more benign but rate-sensitive (US 30-year mortgage ~6.3–6.4%, UK 2-year fixed ~5.0%, with ~1.8mn UK fixed-rate mortgages rolling off in 2026). REITs, by contrast, have staged a sharp rebound — **FTSE Nareit All Equity +14.4% YTD** through late June, reversing a -5.65% 2025 H1, entering the year at their largest P/E discount to the S&P 500 in 25 years. Data-center REITs are the standout sub-sector (+40%+), a direct read-through of the AI capex buildout.

**Managed futures/CTAs:** See §4.5 — genuinely regime-dependent, with the crisis-alpha/diversification case resting on whether 2026's volatility stays sufficiently trend-generating (sustained oil, gold, and FX moves) rather than mean-reverting.

## 6. Long-Term Secular Themes

**AI capex as an infrastructure supercycle.** At ~5% of US GDP, 2026 AI infrastructure spend is being compared by several analysts to the Manhattan Project, Apollo program, and the interstate highway system in relative scale. The bull case draws on railroads, telecom fiber, and early cloud computing — all of which "overbuilt" ahead of demand but left durable infrastructure underpinning the next productivity era. The bear counterpoint is equally concrete: the telecom fiber boom destroyed **>$2 trillion in equity value** before eventual utilization caught up, and cloud computing took ~15 years to generate sustained returns. Enterprise adoption data supports genuine caution here: 80–90% of firms report using AI in at least one function, but **fewer than 40% have scaled beyond pilots** — the productivity payoff that would justify current capex levels is not yet demonstrated at scale. One mitigating factor is that the largest hyperscalers are still substantially self-funding capex from operating cash flow rather than external debt, which has historically produced softer landings than debt-financed infrastructure booms — though the GPU-backed debt maturities coming due in 2026–2027 (originated 2023–2025, coinciding with peak depreciation on those same GPU vintages) complicate that reassurance at the margin.

**Deglobalization and reshoring.** The 2026 pattern is not full decoupling but "selective diversification" — China+1/China+2 strategies that retain existing China capacity while adding secondary sourcing, with Mexico now holding 16.3% of US trade (the top US trading partner since 2023). 67% of surveyed companies now treat tariff exposure as a *permanent* structural risk requiring durable network redesign, not a temporary shock to wait out — which reinforces the inflation-persistence argument in §1.1: friend-shoring trades geopolitical resilience for higher structural unit costs via duplicated capacity and reduced scale efficiency.

**Demographics.** China's population has shrunk by ~1mn/year since 2022; its working-age share has fallen to 61.89% (from 67.33% a decade ago), with the IMF estimating aging could shave 0.3pp/year off TFP growth now, rising to 1.3pp/year of GDP growth drag from 2035. The dominant sell-side narrative is that AI-driven automation offsets this demographic drag — but the empirical evidence specifically testing this (IMF work on Japan) is mixed: Japanese workers show lower AI exposure than peer economies, and industry-level data shows no clear evidence that aging itself drives faster robot/automation adoption. What has empirically worked in Japan is rising labor-force participation among women and seniors — a labor-supply adaptation, not a productivity/automation story. **This nuance matters: "AI will offset demographics" should be treated as the dominant narrative, not a proven mechanism.**

**Energy transition.** Global energy capex is ~$3.4tn in 2026 (+5% YoY), with clean energy investment (~$2.2tn) now running nearly 2x fossil fuel extraction capex (~$1.2tn) for the first time. But at the margin, the picture is more nuanced: AI/data-center incremental power demand is disproportionately being met by natural gas and a nuclear revival (~15 reactors, ~12GW coming online in 2026, reversing a prior year of net nuclear contraction) rather than renewables — one estimate has planned non-renewable capacity additions growing 71% year-over-year while renewable capacity growth has flattened to ~2%. These two data points are not contradictory; they reflect different scopes (whole-economy capex vs. the specific incremental generation being built for new AI load) and should be presented as a nuance rather than smoothed into a single "clean energy is winning" number.

**Fiscal dominance as a regime shift.** Covered in §1.4 — the convergence of BlackRock, PIMCO, Bridgewater and IMF language in 2026 is itself a notable data point, arguing for treating higher term premia and elevated inflation/fiscal risk premia as a structural feature of the coming years rather than a cyclical anomaly to be waited out.
